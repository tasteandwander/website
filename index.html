Roman food doesn't come from a menu — it comes from a neighbourhood. Each rione has its own history, its own poverty, its own pride. The Jewish Ghetto gave Rome its fried artichoke. Testaccio, built around a slaughterhouse, gave it offal. Trastevere kept the old kitchens alive when the rest of the city moved on.

Below are the six neighbourhoods that matter most if you want to eat well. Pick yours, or let the map find you.

The golden rules of eating in Rome

Rule 01 — Carbonara
No cream. Never cream. Guanciale, eggs, Pecorino, black pepper. If the menu says pancetta, walk out calmly.
Rule 02 — Timing
Romans eat lunch at 1:30pm and dinner at 8:30pm. Arriving at 7pm signals tourist. Arriving at 8pm signals effort.
Rule 03 — Saturday
Trippa alla Romana is a Saturday dish. If you see it on a Tuesday, either it's frozen or the place knows tourists won't know.
Rule 04 — Pizza
Roman pizza is thin and crisp. Thick dough is Neapolitan and beautiful, but ordering it in Rome and calling it Roman is an affront.
Rule 05 — Coffee
You stand at the bar. You drink it in three sips. You do not order a cappuccino after 11am if you want to be taken seriously.
Rule 06 — The Bill
Coperto (cover charge) is normal and legal. Bread being brought without asking means you're paying for it. No complaints.

The offal question

Quinto Quarto
The "fifth quarter" — offal, intestines, tail, tripe. This was slaughterhouse workers' wages. It became Testaccio's identity. Try one dish. Start with trippa.
Start here
Trippa alla Romana: tripe in tomato with mint and Pecorino. The flavour is rich and bright, not confrontational. It's the gentlest entry into offal Rome.
The brave move
Rigatoni alla Pajata — pasta with veal intestines that still contain the mother's milk. Earthy, intense, entirely Roman. Available in very few places now.

What to eat here

🌿
Carciofi alla Giudia
The jewel of the Ghetto. Artichoke deep-fried whole until the petals crisp like chips while the heart stays tender. Simple, perfect, unrepeatable.
🌸
Fiori di Zucca
Zucchini flowers stuffed with mozzarella and anchovy, battered and fried. The Ghetto serves the best version in the city.
🐟
Baccalà fritto
Salt cod in a light batter, fried golden. The Jewish kitchen's answer to Lent — now a year-round Roman staple.
🥧
Crostata Ricotta e Visciole
Pastry filled with sheep's ricotta and sour cherry jam — a Jewish-Roman dessert of remarkable subtlety. Find it at Roscioli or in the Ghetto bakeries.

The seasonal rule

Artichoke season
Carciofi alla Giudia is at its absolute best from February to May, when the Romanesco artichoke is in season. Out of season, politely decline.
Shabbat timing
Many restaurants in the Ghetto close for Shabbat (Friday evening through Saturday). Plan accordingly — some of the best spots won't be open Saturday lunch.
Kosher rules
Many Ghetto restaurants are kosher — no mixing of meat and dairy. Don't be surprised when Pecorino doesn't come with the meat dishes. It's intentional and correct.

Rione I · The Oldest

Monti

Rome's first rione — ancient, bohemian, and increasingly good at wine. Independent enotece sit next to traditional restaurants that have barely changed since the 1970s. The best of both Romes.

What to eat here

🍷
Natural wine + cicchetti
Monti leads Rome's natural wine scene. Small plates alongside orange wines and skin-contact whites — a new Roman ritual that pairs beautifully with old recipes.
🍝
Cacio e Pepe
Several small trattorias here do a very serious cacio e pepe in a neighbourhood that hasn't been ruined by tourism yet.
🫙
Fagioli con le Cotiche
Beans and pork rind — the ultimate peasant dish, now served in Monti's more honest trattorias. Warming, ancient, entirely Roman.

The Monti approach

Aperitivo hour
Monti has the best aperitivo scene in Rome. From 6–8pm, small enotece serve snacks with natural wine at prices that make the rest of the city look embarrassed.
Avoid the main square
Piazza della Madonna dei Monti is beautiful but its restaurants are tourist-facing. Walk one block in any direction to find the real neighbourhood.
